Saltbrine Reach

The tide comes in twice a day and takes something each time, and nobody keeps count anymore.

History

Saltbrine Reach is a curve of coast, a crescent harbour, a string of fishing villages, and a long-running argument with the sea. The argument has been running since the first Reach settlement was founded, by fishermen whose names have not survived, on a beach where a freshwater stream met the salt water and the mackerel schooled thick enough to catch with your hands. That beach is gone now. The sea took it. The settlement moved inland, and then inland again, and the current harbour is the sixth to have held the Reach's name, and the seventh will be built, in time, because it always is.

The Reach is a pirate coast in the careful, formal sense that the crown has never been able to enforce its ordinary laws here. The Harbourmaster — currently Cole, a stressed and competent man who has held the post for twenty years — is a crown appointee, but his actual authority is limited by the fact that he does not pay the dockworkers' wages and cannot enforce any law that would interrupt the fishing. The dockworkers' wages are paid by the fishing cooperatives, which are paid by the markets, which are paid, in the end, by the guilds of the Heartlands, who buy Reach-caught salmon and lobster and swordfish in quantities the Reach could not replace with any other buyer. This is the polity. Cole manages it. He does not enforce it.

The pirates, therefore, are tolerated. Not by Cole, but by the economics. A small cove at the eastern edge of the harbour holds a settlement that no map shows and everyone knows the location of, and the pirates there pay a tithe to the fishing cooperatives in exchange for not robbing the cooperatives' boats. When the tithe is paid, the pirates rob other boats — trade vessels from further down the coast, slow galleons out of kingdoms the Reach does not recognise. When the tithe is not paid, for whatever reason, the pirates rob the cooperatives, and the cooperatives pay the tithe again. This system is stable. Cole would like to dismantle it. He has not been given the force to do so, and he has begun to suspect that the force has been withheld on purpose.

The sea itself is the other party to the Reach's long argument. The sea gives. The sea takes. The oyster beds at the northern end of the harbour produce pearls that are sold inland for hundreds of coins each, and two boats a year are lost in the oyster channels. The deep-water grounds past the breakwater yield swordfish and shark, and the siren rocks just past them yield shipwrecks on the schedule of one every eight or nine years. None of this is unusual for a fishing coast. What is unusual, what has begun to be unusual, is that the sea has started to sing.

The singing has been audible for about a year. Fishmonger Mara noticed it first: a low note at the bottom of the harbour tide, carrying up through the hulls of the moored boats on a calm night. She asked three other fishermen, and they had all heard it and had all assumed they were imagining it. They were not imagining it. Something is under the harbour. The oldest among the fishermen, a man named Petric who will not speak his surname, says it is the Kraken, and that the Kraken used to live farther out, and that it has come closer because something has changed, and that nothing good ever comes of the Kraken coming closer. Petric has been saying this for forty years and has been wrong for thirty-nine of them. He is not wrong now.

Now

The pirates blockaded the harbour last season and were driven off by a concerted effort that cost three boats and two captains. The blockade has not reformed, but the pirate cove is rebuilding, and the cooperatives are paying a higher tithe than they were. Mara has set up a fishing tackle and food stall at the dock and is quietly organising a delegation to travel to Moryskah to consult the Bog Witch on the singing. Harbourmaster Cole is three nights into a week without sleep and has a stack of unanswered letters from the crown on his desk. The Kraken is closer. The tide comes in twice a day.

Geography

A curved harbour protected by a long stone breakwater. Beach to either side. Fishing villages running up the coast, each with its own jetty. A pirate cove to the east, informally. Deep water past the breakwater, where the siren rocks are. The harbour town proper has a small market, a range, a bank, and Mara's tackle shop.

Factions

  • The Fishing Cooperatives — The actual government of the Reach in all practical senses. A consortium of boat captains and fishmongers who pay the dockworkers and negotiate with the pirates. Mara is on the inner council. (friendly if you bring them fish, transactional if you don't, loyal if you help them with a problem they name themselves)
  • The Harbourmaster's Office — Cole, two clerks, and a ledger that does not balance. The Office collects docking fees and issues writs. Its actual authority is narrow. Its informal role is to absorb blame for anything the cooperatives do not want to be blamed for. (grateful for competence, quietly desperate for allies)
  • The Pirate Cove — A shanty settlement of buccaneers, privateers, and assorted unlicensed seafarers. Led informally by a rotating cast of captains who do not publicly acknowledge a leader but who all answer the same horn. (hostile on principle, negotiable in practice, capable of hiring you if you prove competent and discreet)
  • The Deep-Water Watchers — A handful of old fishermen, Petric chief among them, who have begun meeting at dusk on the breakwater and not speaking, and watching the water. (they will not speak to you until you have heard the singing; after that, they will speak to nobody else)

Landmarks

  • Saltbrine Harbour — The main dock. Five jetties. A bank. A range for cooking fresh catch at dockside. Mara's stall is nearest the market. (secret: The north jetty's third piling is older than the others by about two hundred years. Nobody knows why it was reused.)
  • The Pirate Cove — A hidden harbour to the east, accessible by a tide-dependent channel. A cluster of shanties, a galleon in permanent dry dock, a tavern. (secret: The galleon's hull is not wood. Every timber of it was replaced, plank by plank, over the last hundred years, and the original ship it was is no longer present in any meaningful way, but the name on the stern has not changed.)
  • The Siren Rocks — A cluster of jagged black rocks past the breakwater. The sirens nest here. Ships that sail close do not always return. (secret: The rocks are hollow. The sirens live inside them, not on them. The hollows are large.)
  • The Oyster Beds — A shallow bed at the northern end of the harbour, farmed by the cooperatives. Pearls. Sea glass. Occasional coral fragments. (secret: One of the oysters — no one knows which — has been growing continuously for a century. Its pearl is a relic.)
  • Mara's Stall — A plank counter by the dock. Raw fish on ice. Cooked fish for travellers. A stove. A ledger. (secret: The ledger records not only sales but everything Mara has heard from the sailors who bought from her, and it is kept in code only the cooperatives can read.)

Atmosphere

  • Weather: salt wind year-round, fog at dawn, hot summer afternoons, winter storms that can rearrange the jetties
  • Colors: grey-blue sea, wet dark wood, pale rope, copper verdigris on the bells, the red of the pirate cove's flags; gull white everywhere
  • Music: a concertina and a fiddle over a slow swell of cello — a harbour song that remembers drowned singers without dwelling on them
