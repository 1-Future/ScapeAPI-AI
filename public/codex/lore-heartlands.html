The Heartlands

Where a bell still rings at noon and the roads are mostly paved.

History

Before the Heartlands were the Heartlands, they were nothing — a river plain between two low ranges, grazed by aurochs and crossed by nomads who left no name. The first permanent settlement was a waystation called Aldren's Rest, built by a band of retired sellswords who decided they were too old to die useful deaths and too young to stop eating. Aldren himself is remembered in no song, but his well still stands, half-buried behind the Heartlands market square, and children sometimes drop copper coins down it on feast days.

The kingdom proper coalesced during what the scholars call the Granary Centuries — three hundred years when the harvests were freakishly, almost suspiciously generous, and the surplus drew craftsmen, priests, and opportunists the way a fresh loaf draws flies. By the time the weather returned to normal, the Heartlands had guilds, a crown, a mint, a tax code, and the conviction that it had always been this way. The conviction is the most durable of those achievements. Every other kingdom that has risen on Aelgard has looked at the Heartlands and thought: that is what permanence ought to feel like.

It was not, of course, permanent. The Spellfire War burned Old Aldren to the foundations and scattered the original noble houses so thoroughly that three of the current baronies are held by the descendants of kitchen staff. The crown rebuilt, because the crown always rebuilds, and the rebuilt city has the odd lopsided charm of a thing pieced together from memory. The bell tower at the centre of the market is the third to stand on that spot, and the bell itself is the second recast of a bell that melted when the wizards took the walls. Nobody mentions this. Every noon, the bell rings, and the carters pull their wagons aside, and everyone agrees, without saying so, that this is how a city is supposed to sound.

The Heartlands is not the oldest kingdom on Aelgard and it is not the strongest. What it is, is the most comfortable. The roads are paved — not all of them, and not well, but paved enough that a cart can reach the Veilwood border in three days without a broken axle. The guilds will train you if you can pay and gate you if you cannot, which is what guilds everywhere do, but at least here the rules are written down. The priests of the Last Light keep a flame burning in every chapel in every village, and when the flame goes out, a Lamplighter replaces it, and nobody asks too many questions about why this matters. It matters because it has always mattered.

If you arrive here as an outsider, the first thing you will notice is that the people are busy without being frantic. The second thing you will notice is that nobody watches the sky for what might be coming. They have forgotten how. They are not wrong to have forgotten — the Heartlands is genuinely, specifically safe, ringed by its guard patrols and its paved roads and its ordinary weather. But step out of it, in any direction, and the world remembers things the Heartlands has chosen not to.

Now

The crown is currently held by a council of three regents, because the last queen died without naming an heir and the three most plausible claimants agreed, after an awkward week, that none of them actually wanted the job. The arrangement has held for eleven years, which is eleven years longer than anyone predicted. The regents argue in public and agree in private, and the city runs.

Tension lives in the guilds more than in the palace. The Smiths want more tin than the mines are producing. The Apothecaries say the Farming Guild is hoarding ranarr. The Lamplighters — a strange, old order that everyone forgets to count as a guild until a flame goes out — are quietly grieving: three of their senior members vanished in the last year, and nobody has returned from looking for them. Walk into the market and you will see all of this layered: the shout of the grain criers, the slow patrol of the guard, and the one Lamplighter on the corner, holding a candle that is always lit, watching the road north.

Geography

A broad river plain bracketed by low grass hills, with a river — the Aldrin — running roughly east-west and meeting the sea far to the southwest. The soil is deep and tillable. Orchards climb the southern slopes. North of the city, the land rises into scrub that eventually becomes the Wilds. To the east, a paved road threads through hills toward the Sootworks; to the west, another reaches the Veilwood.

Climate is temperate. Summers are warm and long, winters short and damp. Storms come in from the southwest in spring.

Factions

  • The Three Regents — A council of three — a merchant, a general, and a priest — who rule the Heartlands by mutual reluctance since the last queen died heirless. They hold formal court once a month and informal court every night over wine. (polite to anyone with manners, generous to anyone who completes a Heartlands quest, wary of adventurers who ask too many questions about the northern border)
  • The Lamplighters Guild — An old religious order that tends the Last Light — a sacred flame kept burning in every Heartlands chapel. They claim the flame was lit from a coal taken from Aelgard's first hearth, and that if it ever goes out in every chapel at once, something specific and bad will happen. They will not say what. (warm if you ever light a flame in a chapel, cold if you ever extinguish one, and they notice either way)
  • The Six Guilds — Smiths, Fishers, Cooks, Miners, Warriors, and Magi — the backbone of Heartlands commerce and training. Entry is gated by skill and modest fees. The guildmasters feud politely and protect each other ferociously when outsiders criticize any of them. (transactional at first, then quietly loyal once you've earned your medallion)
  • The Grain Criers — The daily-price-shouters of the market square. Not officially a faction, but they know everything that moves in or out of the Heartlands, because they watch prices for a living. (friendly if tipped, invaluable if courted)

Landmarks

  • The Chapel of the Last Light — The oldest chapel in the city, one narrow stone nave with a flame that has not gone out since the city was rebuilt. The current priest is very old and has not left the chapel in thirty years. (secret: Behind the altar stone, if you've completed The Culinaromancer's Curse and the priest has come to trust you, a loose slab lifts onto a narrow stair down. What is down there is not explained to anyone who has not been down before.)
  • The Grand Hall — A teleport hub linking the six Heartlands guilds under one vaulted roof. Built into the hill behind the palace. Opens only after the Grand Feast has been survived. (secret: The six guildmasters each have a private alcove here, and the alcoves are not the same size. The Cook's is largest. Nobody will explain why.)
  • The Old Mill — A flour mill on the Aldrin, abandoned after the miller drowned in his own wheel. Ghost-touched grain still grows in the field behind it; the crop is richer, and the harvest smells faintly of river water. (secret: The miller is not entirely gone. On certain nights, the wheel turns without wind or current, and the flour that comes out is whiter than anything a living mill can produce.)
  • The Noble Mansion Row — A crescent of stone houses north of the palace where the remaining old-blood families live. The rows are patrolled but porous — a thief with nerve and a good route can make a fortune here in a single night. (secret: One of the five houses is empty. It has been empty for four generations, and the family's name has been carefully removed from every record. The door opens only with a key that has never been found.)
  • The Aldrin Crossing — The stone bridge at the centre of the city. Five arches, built in one piece after the Spellfire War. Traders gather at the north end every morning. (secret: The fourth arch from the east, if you crouch under it at low water, shows a carved sigil that does not match the mason's marks on the others. The sigil is faint but it is not weathered.)

Atmosphere

  • Weather: temperate and reliable, long summer evenings, damp autumn mornings, short winters that frost the orchards but rarely the roads
  • Colors: worn sandstone, wheat gold, slate blue river, dark oak timber, the cream-and-red of Heartlands banners; lanterns at dusk turn the whole city amber
  • Music: a slow hammered string against a hearth-warm drone — familiar enough to feel like coming home, patient enough to suggest that home has been waiting
